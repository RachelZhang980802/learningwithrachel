01

CONCEPTS / DEFINE THE WORDS

First, the words.

What do we mean when we speak of culture and civilization?

同一个概念，要放回作者的研究语境。

The words and overlap, but they are not used in exactly the same way by every writer. A dictionary, an anthropologist and a political theorist may each be asking a different question.

CULTURE

A shared way of life.

Culture includes the beliefs, customs, knowledge and practices people learn and share. It appears in art, but also in everyday habits, celebrations and judgments about what matters.

Word history: Latin cultura is associated with cultivation. The slide’s cultus belongs to this longer word history; it is not the whole derivation. Dictionary ↗

CIVILIZATION

A question of scale.

Civilization can refer to forms of social organization or to broad historical and cultural identities. Its relationship to culture depends on the writer’s purpose; the two terms do not always form a simple hierarchy.

Word history: civil is related to Latin civilis and civis, “citizen.” This connection suggests public life, but does not by itself define modern civilization. Dictionary ↗

EDWARD BURNETT TYLOR · 1871

What does culture contain?

In Primitive Culture, Tylor treats culture or civilization, in its broad ethnographic sense, as a complex whole. It includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom, and abilities and habits acquired as a member of society.

Focus: the contents of culture and how people acquire them.

Read the original work ↗

SAMUEL P. HUNTINGTON · 1993 / 1996

Where do people belong?

Huntington’s civilizational framework emphasizes broad cultural belonging: religion, history, language, customs and self-identification. He uses these connections to interpret international relations and potential conflicts.

Focus: the boundaries of identity and their political significance.

MYTH RETURNS

Venus comes ashore.

Classical myths are retold in later art. In The Birth of Venus, the goddess arrives at the shore; the body, wind and flowing fabric invite us to observe beauty and movement.

The Uffizi leaves room for more than one identification of the accompanying figures. Zephyr’s companion may be Aura; the woman on the right has been read as a Grace or the Hora of spring. Interpretation should preserve such uncertainty.

03

IDEAS & DIALOGUE / COMPARE PERSPECTIVES

Beyond admiration.

How should we relate cultural achievement, power and coexistence?

欣赏文明成果，也要比较历史解释与权力关系。

The move from art to the Spanish Inquisition, the slave trade, colonization and the Holocaust changes our perspective. Cultural achievement and historical violence both belong in our account of the past.

These histories involve different periods, institutions and actors. Specify who acted, against whom, and through which mechanisms. Do not turn distinct events into a timeless judgment about an entire civilization.

The political cartoon China — The Cake of Kings and Emperors, dated 1898 in the slides, presents China as a cake to be divided. It invites analysis of imperial rivalry. When linking it to the Opium Wars, distinguish the dates and events.

Who speaks in this image — and who is spoken for?

Three different questions.

Fukuyama, Huntington and Nye offer different starting points. The first two address historical direction and patterns of conflict; the third examines a mechanism of influence.

01

Fukuyama

The end of history?

His argument concerns liberal democracy and ideological development. “The end of history” does not mean that events stop happening. The slide’s additional “only one way” sentence should not be treated as a verified direct quotation.

02

Huntington

The clash of civilizations?

His framework foregrounds cultural and religious identities. Ask where interests, institutions, internal differences and particular histories complicate an explanation based on civilizational boundaries.

03

Nye

Soft power

His concept concerns the ability to influence preferences through attraction. It is not a synonym for every diplomatic or publicity campaign: the response of the audience matters.

Modernization
and Westernization.

Distinguish changes in technology, institutions and everyday life from the adoption of a particular cultural identity. Comparing these dimensions makes it possible to ask what changes and what remains continuous.A discussion proposition from W1, p.31现代化与西方化需要分开定义、分别举证。

STUDY GUIDE / BUILD A READING PRACTICE

Read with a question.

Move from recognizing information to making a reasoned response.

预习提问 → 课堂连接 → 课后举证。

Use a repeatable cycle: prepare a question, connect the concepts, and return to the evidence. A useful notebook records not only an answer, but also where it comes from.

  1. BEFORE

    Prepare the language.

    Read titles and subtitles. Look up words that block understanding. Record one definition and one question before the lesson.

  2. DURING

    Make connections.

    Link people, works and concepts. Note the period, the claim and its evidence. Separate quotations, explanations and your own judgments.

  3. AFTER

    Return to the evidence.

    Complete the exercises on time. Restate an idea using the English key terms; check its source and consider a possible counterexample.

Reading in English: support without dependence

“Walking Stick Dependency Syndrome” is the lecturer’s metaphor for reliance on translation, not an established diagnosis. Interlanguage and fossilization are separate terms from second-language research; they should not be explained through an unsupported picture of English and Chinese stored in isolated compartments.

Try to grasp the main idea in English. Use Chinese where it clarifies a difficult point, then return to the English wording. Using a translation is not a reason for shame.
